# A signed AI maintenance finding still needs a human authority check

**Status:** Draft for review, 2026-10-12. Proposed primary query: `AI aircraft maintenance sign-off`; search metrics **Not measured**. Reader: MRO engineering or quality lead. Intent: separate a model finding, a human finding disposition, and a maintenance release. Business objective: test whether a per-finding record makes an existing review process easier to audit.

**Supporting entities:** artifact hashing; Ed25519; certifying-staff authorisation; EASA Part-145; 14 CFR 43.9. **Product connection:** draft MRO AI Sign-off Card. **Publication gate:** legal and maintenance-process review for the intended jurisdiction, plus verified public spec URL.

Suppose an image-analysis model marks a possible anomaly in a borescope image. A maintenance organisation has at least three distinct questions. Which exact image and model version produced the observation? What did a person decide about that observation? Who is authorised to make the maintenance record and any release decision under the organisation's procedures? A single green “AI passed” label answers none of them.

The draft **MRO AI Sign-off Card v0.1** records one finding. `finding` identifies the model and its version, the finding kind, a summary, and a numeric confidence value from 0 to 1. The format does not define that number as a calibrated probability. `artifact.sha256` binds the record to the exact source bytes, without normalising image or text content. `evidence_refs` points to supporting material. `human_review` records a pseudonymous staff ID, the decision on the **finding** (`accept`, `override`, or `reject`), a time, and a pointer to an external authority record. An override needs a reason.

The card also has an **embedded** Ed25519 signature. The draft signing profile removes only `signature.signature_b64url`, canonicalises the remaining card with RFC 8785, hashes it with SHA-256, and signs the digest. A verifier needs a separately trusted public-key registry and must bind the expected key ID to the right person or organisation. The signature establishes that a trusted key holder signed those bytes. It says nothing by itself about the truth of the finding, the person's privileges, or whether the aircraft may return to service.

That boundary matches the real maintenance context. [EASA's continuing-airworthiness rules](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/document-library/easy-access-rules/online-publications/easy-access-rules-continuing-airworthiness?erules-id=ERULES-1963177438-272) describe certification authorisations and their scope under Part-145. [14 CFR 43.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-43/section-43.9) specifies U.S. maintenance-record content in its applicable scope. A card could be attached to a controlled workflow, but it is not the organisation's certificate of release to service or a replacement for its required records.

The interesting failure cases are mundane. The referenced image might have changed, so the digest must be recomputed from authorised bytes. The model version might be mislabelled. A signing key might be expired or revoked. A staff member might be authorised for a different task or aircraft type. The card's local schema can catch missing fields and some conflicting values; it cannot look up current staff privileges or interpret the maintenance manual.

A safe pilot uses fictional images and people. Run the schema validator on a valid card, change one signed field and observe verification fail, then remove or revoke the test key from a local trust record and observe that the workflow refuses to treat the signature as trusted. Keep a separate, explicit human step for the actual maintenance decision. No real aircraft finding belongs in a public pull request or blog example.

**CTA for publication:** Review the draft card against one existing internal finding workflow and identify the external authority and record systems it would need; do not use the draft to release an aircraft.
